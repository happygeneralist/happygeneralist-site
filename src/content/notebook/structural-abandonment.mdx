## Definition

Structural abandonment refers to the systemic withdrawal, fragmentation or failure of institutional supports that shifts the burden of survival onto individuals.

It captures how health, social and economic infrastructures often exist in policy and rhetoric but collapse in practice, delivering piecemeal or “marginal” interventions that force citizens to act as project managers of their own survival.

The concept emphasises both the structural nature of neglect and the lived consequences of navigating survival without a coherent scaffold of support.

## Lineage

**Urban and policy studies:** Draws inspiration from organised abandonment (Gilmore, 2007; Harvey, 2003), which describes deliberate patterns of disinvestment in communities.

**Anthropology of neglect:** Resonates with João Biehl’s (2005) zones of social abandonment, which document institutional and cultural withdrawal from marginalised lives.

**Public administration:** Extends critiques of the hollow state (Milward and Provan, 2000), where governance is outsourced and fragmented, often leaving citizens to navigate incoherent service ecosystems.

**Built environment metaphor:** Re-appropriates the architectural use of “structural abandonment” in domicology (LaMore et al., 2015), where abandoned buildings symbolise the decay of scaffolding, applied here to social infrastructures.

## Distinctions

**From organised abandonment:** focuses not on collective geographies of neglect but on individual survival scaffolds.

**From hollow state:** moves beyond institutional analysis of contracting and outsourcing to centre the citizen’s embodied experience of systemic fragmentation.

**From social abandonment:** is not limited to extreme marginalisation; instead, it highlights how abandonment manifests even within supposedly “universal” service systems.

## Applications

**Public policy outcomes:** Offers a diagnostic lens for evaluating whether services provide scaffolds or merely fragmented, marginal supports.

**Service design:** Encourages practitioners to design beyond “patchwork fixes” toward structures that reduce citizen survival labour.

**Advocacy and ethics:** Reframes failure from the individual — “unable to cope” — to the system: “failed to scaffold survival”, shifting accountability.

<section class="notebook-grid" aria-label="Structural abandonment implications">
  <section class="notebook-panel">
    <h2>Additional dynamics</h2>

    <h3>Administrative burden as survival labour</h3>
    <p>Citizens must invest enormous time, energy and literacy into navigating fragmented systems.</p>
    <p>Instead of scaffolding, institutions hand over work: forms, eligibility proofs and appeals, effectively shifting costs to those least resourced.</p>

    <h3>Fragmented micro-interventions</h3>
    <p>Supports are delivered as isolated fixes, such as a referral, voucher or six-session therapy limit, rather than integrated scaffolds.</p>
    <p>This creates a paradox: the system appears responsive but structurally fails to meet needs.</p>

    <h3>Conditional reciprocity</h3>
    <p>Social and institutional supports often hinge on compliance, performance or “worthiness” tests, such as work requirements or disability reassessments.</p>
    <p>This creates cycles of precarity where support can be revoked, intensifying insecurity.</p>

    <h3>Invisible costs of translation</h3>
    <p>Individuals must constantly translate their needs into institutional language: diagnoses, benefit codes and “acceptable” narratives.</p>
    <p>This translation burden further entrenches inequity: those who cannot perform it are excluded.</p>

    <h3>Systemic gaslighting</h3>
    <p>Citizens are told supports exist, but experience consistent failure in access and delivery.</p>
    <p>The gap between rhetoric — “universal access” — and reality produces alienation and mistrust.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="notebook-panel">
    <h2>Policy implications</h2>

    <h3>From patchwork to scaffold</h3>
    <p>Current systems deliver marginal gains, or band-aid fixes, rather than holistic scaffolding.</p>
    <p>Policy evaluation should shift from counting outputs to assessing scaffold sufficiency: whether combined supports create livability.</p>

    <h3>Reducing citizen burden</h3>
    <p>Policies should account for the administrative, emotional and translation labour imposed on citizens.</p>
    <p>Survival labour audits could track and reduce the hidden costs of accessing support.</p>

    <h3>Integration versus siloing</h3>
    <p>Fragmented service silos across health, housing and employment create abandonment by failing to coordinate.</p>
    <p>Policy design should prioritise cross-sector integration and “no wrong door” access points.</p>

    <h3>Accountability for withdrawal</h3>
    <p>Structural abandonment thrives on invisibility: supports are quietly withdrawn or underfunded.</p>
    <p>Public policy should require transparency when scaffolds are reduced, including reporting service withdrawal as a policy outcome.</p>

    <h3>Equity lens</h3>
    <p>Abandonment is not evenly distributed. It disproportionately affects marginalised groups, including disabled people, racialised communities and low-income households.</p>
    <p>Policy must explicitly map and redress these unequal abandonment patterns.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="notebook-panel">
    <h2>Service design implications</h2>

    <h3>Designing against burden</h3>
    <p>Service designers can ask: who is carrying the hidden work here — the system or the citizen?</p>
    <p>Design interventions should shift labour away from citizens and back into the institution.</p>

    <h3>Scaffold mapping</h3>
    <p>Instead of mapping only “user journeys”, map where scaffolding collapses across touchpoints.</p>
    <p>This reveals where survival is being project-managed by the individual.</p>

    <h3>Whole-life integration</h3>
    <p>Services should be designed not as isolated offerings but as interconnected scaffolds that support real-world complexity: health, income, housing and care.</p>

    <h3>Language and translation</h3>
    <p>Service design can reduce translation costs by adopting plain language, citizen-centred communication and adaptive intake processes.</p>

    <h3>Measuring livability, not just usability</h3>
    <p>Usability testing in service design often focuses on efficiency of a touchpoint.</p>
    <p>With structural abandonment as a lens, design should evaluate whether services contribute to livability, not just successful transactions.</p>
  </section>
