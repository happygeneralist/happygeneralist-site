## Holding pattern

This is a holding page for a larger cluster of thinking about user needs: how they are discovered, written, governed, distorted, erased, superseded and connected to civic outcomes.

It should eventually become the equivalent of the Pathologies of Practice holding area, but focused specifically on user needs and their failure modes.

## Status of these terms

The terms in this cluster are Happygeneralist working definitions.

They are part of the theoretical underpinning of Civic Design Intelligence. They should be useful enough to publish as public Notebook entries, but they should not be presented as settled external standards or widely established design terminology.

They exist to reduce drift in the wider body of work: to make sure future tools, labs and essays use these ideas consistently while still allowing them to evolve.

## Why this cluster exists

User needs are often treated as small delivery artefacts: statements for backlogs, content pages, service assessments or product decisions.

That is useful, but too narrow.

In public-service work, user needs sit inside a larger ecology of evidence, civic obligations, institutional constraints, policy choices, lived experience and operational realities. If that ecology is not made visible, user needs can become decorative: present in language, weak in power.

## Core distinction

A user need names what a person must be able to accomplish, understand, decide, access, maintain or resolve.

A civic need names what the system or institution must make possible, protect or repair.

A central problem in this cluster is that these concepts are often collapsed into tasks, features, content, policy aims, outcomes or organisational priorities.

## Early pattern map

### User needs erasure

A need is ignored, minimised, reframed, individualised or made illegible inside a policy, project, strategy, speech or service design.

The organisation may still say it is user-centred, but the need no longer has a traceable role in decisions, priorities or outcomes.

### User needs drift

A need remains visible, but its meaning moves away from the evidence, context or outcome that originally made it useful.

The wording may survive while the relationship to lived experience weakens.

### User needs collapse

The distinction between user needs and other concepts breaks down.

A need becomes treated as a task, feature, content requirement, user story, service output, policy aim or organisational priority.

### Civic needs erasure

Civic obligations, justice floors, public value or structural duties are pushed out of view when needs are framed only as individual tasks or transactional service moments.

This is where a user-centred frame can become too small for public-service reality.

### Needs as traceable objects

A more positive pattern: user needs and civic needs are treated as persistent, traceable, supersedable knowledge objects.

They can be linked to evidence, pain points, behaviours, decisions, outcomes and artefacts. They can be reviewed over time instead of rediscovered or rewritten from scratch.

## Relationship to Civic Design Intelligence

Civic Design Intelligence is connected to this cluster because it is trying to solve a practical version of the same problem.

If needs only live in reports, workshops or backlogs, they are easy to lose. If they become structured knowledge objects, they can be inspected, connected and challenged.

The system should help answer questions such as:

- what needs have been identified?
- what evidence supports them?
- which needs are weakly evidenced?
- which needs have been superseded?
- which decisions depend on which needs?
- which pain points or behaviours point to unmet needs?
- where are civic needs being ignored by user-need framing?
- where has a need drifted from evidence into delivery convenience?
- where has a need collapsed into a feature, task or policy aim?
