An organisation says it wants challenge, evidence, agility and learning.

Then someone takes those claims seriously.

They question a decision. They expose a gap between the process on paper and the process in practice. They ask where the evidence is, who has authority, what outcome is being pursued, or why the team is repeating work that should already be known.

The response is not curiosity.

The person becomes the problem.

Their tone is questioned. Their role is narrowed. Their motives are examined. Their experience is reframed as rigidity. A structural concern becomes an interpersonal issue. A challenge to the system becomes a conduct problem for the person carrying it.

I have started thinking of this pattern as **organisational antibodies**.

## A working definition

Organisational antibodies are the self-protective responses through which an institution treats knowledge, behaviour or people that expose a gap between its professed identity and its actual practice as threats to be neutralised rather than signals to be learned from.

The metaphor is provisional. Organisations are not bodies, and people who challenge them are not automatically healthy or correct. But the image captures something important: systems can react defensively to the very information they need in order to improve.

The basic pattern looks like this:

```text
The organisation adopts a valued identity
  -> someone behaves according to that identity
  -> their behaviour exposes a gap between claim and practice
  -> the gap threatens competence, authority, status or belonging
  -> attention moves from the signal to the signal-bearer
  -> the person is corrected, contained or discredited
  -> the organisation avoids deeper learning
  -> the original gap remains
```

## Agile as identity rather than capability

Agile provides a particularly clear example.

An organisation can say it works in an agile way while lacking the conditions that make agility possible: clear decision rights, empowered teams, visible priorities, feedback loops, access to users, iterative planning, honest retrospection and the authority to change direction when evidence demands it.

In that environment, agile language becomes part of the organisation’s identity rather than a description of its operating capability.

Someone with genuine agile experience may then create friction simply by behaving as though the stated model is real. They ask for clarity. They expose dependencies. They question work that has no defined outcome. They expect decisions to follow evidence. They treat plans as hypotheses rather than commitments carved into stone.

Those behaviours do not merely challenge a process. They reveal that the organisation may not be what it says it is.

The system then faces a choice:

1. examine the discrepancy and change;
2. defend the existing identity by redefining the challenger as difficult.

Organisational antibodies describe the second response.

The organisation protects the performance of transformation from transformation itself.

## From claim to claimant

The most important move is the transfer of scrutiny.

A useful challenge begins with a claim:

- this decision has no evidence behind it;
- this process contradicts the stated method;
- this risk is being displaced;
- this outcome is not being measured;
- this work has already been done;
- this governance model prevents learning.

A defensive system does not have to answer the claim if it can make the claimant less credible.

The conversation shifts:

- Why are they so negative?
- Why can’t they be more collaborative?
- Is this really their role?
- Why are they making people uncomfortable?
- Are they too attached to one methodology?
- Do they understand how things work here?

Some of these questions can be legitimate. Expertise does not excuse poor behaviour. A challenger can be wrong, abrasive, mistimed or unaware of constraints.

The diagnostic question is whether the substantive claim is still tested fairly, or whether concern about the person becomes a way to avoid examining the system.

## What the response protects

Organisational antibodies may protect more than individual egos.

They can protect:

- informal hierarchies;
- leaders’ identities as competent or progressive;
- previous investments and decisions;
- social cohesion built on avoiding challenge;
- role boundaries that keep authority predictable;
- reputational claims about innovation or maturity;
- the comfort of people who benefit from the current equilibrium.

Some of these are legitimate things to protect. Organisations need coordination, authority, continuity and behavioural boundaries.

The problem begins when protection prevents the organisation from receiving corrective knowledge.

## The hidden cost of attacking the signal

The immediate cost is often carried by the person who spoke.

They spend energy translating, softening, documenting and defending what should have been evaluated on its merits. They may mask, withdraw, burn out or leave. Their expertise remains useful to the organisation only when it does not challenge the organisation’s account of itself.

But the larger cost belongs to the system.

Other people learn not to speak. Weak decisions survive longer. Capability gaps remain hidden. Leadership receives increasingly filtered information. The organisation loses the people most able to detect contradictions, then interprets the resulting silence as evidence that everything is working.

A system can look calmer while becoming less intelligent.

## Not every immune response is pathological

The metaphor has an important limitation.

Resistance can be useful. Organisations should reject fashionable transformations, weak evidence, role overreach and changes that create more harm than value. A manager may need to address someone’s behaviour while still accepting the substance of their critique.

The concept should therefore not be used as a shield against criticism of the challenger.

Possible signs of a genuine antibody response include:

- the substantive claim is never tested;
- scrutiny moves disproportionately to tone, personality, motives or role;
- challenge becomes procedurally or socially costly;
- comparable challenges are treated differently depending on status;
- the response protects a declared identity from contradictory evidence;
- the organisation loses candour or learning capacity as a result;
- the pattern recurs across people and situations.

The question is not whether the organisation agrees with every challenger.

It is whether it can receive uncomfortable information without immediately converting it into a threat.

## A question for organisations

Most organisations have processes for managing resistance to change.

Far fewer have processes for recognising when the organisation itself is resisting knowledge.

That may be the more important capability.

Before asking how to overcome difficult people, it is worth asking:

> What truth might this person be carrying that the organisation has made dangerous to express?

Organisational antibodies is currently a working concept, not a settled theory. It connects to established work on defensive routines, double-loop learning, threat-rigidity, organisational identity, employee voice, silence and psychological safety.

The next task is not to defend the label. It is to test whether the concept helps us see something that those existing traditions leave disconnected: how institutions move scrutiny from a corrective signal to its bearer, who pays the cost, and what repair becomes impossible as a result.
