Organisations often say that they value challenge, evidence, learning, and change. The real test comes when someone acts as though those commitments are true.

A corrective signal may expose a gap between what an organisation says it is and how it actually operates. It may show that a supposedly agile organisation cannot tolerate adaptation, that a learning organisation cannot receive criticism, or that a transformation programme depends on existing authority remaining undisturbed.

At that point, attention can move away from the substance of the challenge. The question is no longer whether the evidence is useful, but whether the person raising it is sufficiently positive, collaborative, senior, tactful, loyal, or within their role.

The provisional phrase **organisational antibodies** describes these self-protective responses. The organisation treats uncomfortable knowledge — or the person carrying it — as something to contain, correct, or neutralise rather than something from which to learn.

The pattern might look like this:

> corrective signal → identity or status threat → scrutiny of the signal-bearer → containment or discrediting → organisational non-learning

This does not mean that every challenger is correct, every disagreement is defensive, or every boundary is retaliation. Organisations need legitimate evaluation, role clarity, and protection from unsuitable change.

The important question is narrower:

> Can the organisation examine an uncomfortable claim without first needing to defeat the person who raised it?

When it cannot, the immediate cost falls on the signal-bearer. The larger cost is hidden: expertise leaves, people learn to remain silent, contradictions become harder to name, and transformation becomes increasingly performative.

The organisation protects the performance of transformation from transformation itself.

Throughout this Notebook, I use **organisational antibodies** as a memorable working metaphor. The underlying phenomenon is closer to what might be described as **identity-protective organisational non-learning**. Whether the metaphor survives further research remains an open question.
